Distributed contacts: springs at rest, coupled in motion

A measured audit of the SR2 sphere surface in MuJoCo: preserved nominal behavior, explicit mass dependence, retained redundant constraints, and a first 6 mm fillet test.

693 declared runs · MuJoCo 3.6.0 + isolated 3.14.0 / Drake 1.57.0 · static and dynamic audit · Read the converted specification

What the solver actually did

The controlled-slip discriminator gives a qualified positive result. With common physical coefficients, a fine timestep and separated tangential damping, the dense 819-sphere model has at most 0.199% normal-force L1 error over the evaluated slip/brake interval, and at most 1.31% median error during sliding. The tests cover plane/cylinder contact at 0.25×, 1× and 4× mass. This approximate agreement requires more than the exact-diagonal flag; coarse high-R variants can diverge.

“The solver collapsed my per-point forces into a few effective constraints … redundant contacts on one body don’t act like parallel springs.”

The observation about coupled, mass-normalized dynamics is supported. The diagnostic below separates force saturation from contact-row reduction: it retains every row, including 32 coincident contacts with a rank-one normal Jacobian. Their aggregate force saturates during unsupported acceleration, but grows linearly with the number of contacts in a supported static equilibrium.

The current fixed mapping is not a transferable material law. At a fixed indentation, force scales directly with object mass in this free-body fixture. The nominal task still reproduces, so this qualifies the mechanical explanation rather than invalidating that particular screwdriver demonstration.

The explicit follow-up now verifies the analytic stiffness prediction and tests a compensated mapping. Normal stiffness becomes mass-independent in the isolated plane/cylinder fixture. The original frozen sweep and nominal chain remain a separate baseline.

The newer-engine extension tests runtime exact inertia, acceleration and controlled slip. Exact inertia alone does not restore physical stiffness. Runtime coefficient translation works, but force fidelity during motion also depends on regularization, friction coupling and timestep.

Spherical fingertips remain the primary design. The additional 6 mm fillet audit tests whether a rounded face/edge/corner transition causes numerical singularities; it is not a replacement fingertip or controller.

At rest, for an interior elliptic-cone contact:
    Rᵢ fᵢ = a_ref,i − (J q̈)ᵢ
    Rᵢ = ((1 − dᵢ) / dᵢ) Λ̂ᵢ
    k_actual,i = 1 / [t_c² (1 − dᵢ) Λ̂ᵢ]
    k_actual,i / k_target,i = Λ_nominal / Λ̂ᵢ

This is the measured static scaling law, not an assertion that arbitrary sliding contacts remain independent springs. Cone-boundary constraints and nonzero accelerations couple the response. MuJoCo 3.6 constraint equations.

Runtime inverse inertia and controlled slip

The requested follow-up was to upgrade an isolated copy, enable diagexact, retain the original sphere parameters, repeat the mass sweep, and then test controlled slip. The upgrade and flag claims are verified; the expectation of automatic physical-stiffness invariance is not supported.

MuJoCo 3.9.0 added the exact-diagonal flag on 27 May 2026. The tested isolated environment uses 3.14.0; the project's pinned 3.6.0 environment is preserved. The efc_diagApprox field became efc_diagA in 3.10. Normal rows are exact when the flag is enabled. Friction-row regularizers receive additional adjustments.

One API caveat matters for this rig: efc_AR is empty under the Newton primal solver in both tested versions, even after mj_projectConstraint. That matrix is assembled for dual solvers. We recover A directly from the exposed Jacobian and mass matrix, without changing solvers:

M = mass_matrix(model, data)  # handles the version-specific mj_fullM API
J = data.efc_J.reshape(data.nefc, model.nv)  # this harness uses dense J
A = J @ numpy.linalg.solve(M, J.T)

Correction to the suggested interpretation: the earlier 3.6 compensation used a compiled body-level approximation, not configuration-dependent inertia. The new adapter genuinely uses the diagonal selected at the current configuration, changes native per-contact solref, then rebuilds the constraint and reference stages before solving. It does not overwrite contact forces.

The exact diagonal is Aᵢᵢ = Jᵢ M⁻¹ Jᵢᵀ. Changing from the approximation to Aᵢᵢ retains mass normalization. With fixed positive solref, stiffness is still 1/[t_c²(1−d₀)Aᵢᵢ]. Scaling all inertias by m scales Aᵢᵢ by 1/m, so fixed parameters still scale stiffness by m.

Maximum relative static error after runtime compensation: 4.44e-16. All five masses are tested against both geometries, with separate forward load runs.

What acceleration and sliding add

The normal interior-cone relation is Rᵢ fᵢ = a_ref,i − Jᵢ q̈. Once coefficients are translated to physical Kᵢ and Cᵢ, it becomes:

fᵢ = Kᵢ δᵢ − Cᵢ vₙ,ᵢ − (Jᵢ q̈)/Rᵢ + γᵢ
Rᵢ = [(1−d₀)/d₀] Λᵢ
γᵢ = force correction from the unilateral/friction-cone boundary

The joint constraint solve determines q̈; its full A contains the rigid-body coupling. Thus exact diagonal compensation guarantees the translated coefficients, not the instantaneous spring force. The benchmark checks the physical unilateral law max(0, Kᵢδᵢ − Cᵢvₙ,ᵢ) against mj_contactForce, sums absolute per-point errors so they cannot cancel, and records the acceleration and cone terms separately.

The sphere pad is fixed and the object has six freely integrated coordinates. Normal loading is 1 N; the acceleration trial adds a 0.5 N load over 20 ms and removes it later. The shear trial ramps a tangential force from zero to 1.2 N, holds, lowers it, and lets the object stop. A shared 5 N·s/m external x damper bounds travel. A y-position servo and 0.5 N·m/rad orientation servo form the declared fixture; neither prescribes slip nor normal indentation. Cylinder motion is along its axis. Friction is 1, all geometry is unchanged, and all dynamic trials use the same 30 ms material relaxation. Logging uses pre-integration position/velocity and the corresponding solved contact forces.

A native route toward independent spring behavior

The additional experiment increases R by reducing d₀ to 0.0001 while analytically holding Kᵢ and Cᵢ fixed. This reduces the acceleration correction. It exposes explicit contact-damping timestep limits: the 0.5 ms trials can oscillate severely, and merely increasing R does not remove cone coupling.

The fine-step variant additionally uses impratio=10000 and separate direct solreffriction. This reduces friction-cone feedback on normal force while retaining the same physical tangential viscous coefficient as the compensated baseline: Cₜ = 10 Cᵢ. Because Rₜ=Rₙ/impratio, its direct friction damping parameter is the normal damping parameter times 10/impratio. Changing impratio alone would change tangential damping. This is a shared analytic translation, not a force fit.

Fine steps are 50 μs at 1×/4× mass and 25 μs at 0.25×; the timestep check halves the nominal step. The friction law is velocity-regularized Coulomb contact. Small motion below the cone boundary is creep, so velocity onset and first cone activation are both reported.

Measured conclusion: the fine-step native variant has normal-load-pulse L1 error up to 0.0002193% and controlled-slip interval error up to 0.1991%. Maximum sliding-frame median error is 1.31%. These are measured bounds for this fixture, not an exact independent-spring theorem.

This artificial duplicate-contact test is a solver diagnostic, not an admissible quadrature of a physical fingertip. Each copy deliberately keeps its full stiffness. It distinguishes rank redundancy, which is expected for forces acting on one rigid body, from deletion or reduction of contact rows.

For identical normal rows under free acceleration, F = n a_ref / (R + n A). A supported equilibrium instead gives F = n a_ref / R. Joint solving can therefore look like “collapse” in the first experiment while preserving distributed static compliance in the second.

Geometry provenance: the 6 mm fillet is the user’s hardware measurement. The older sharp box is obsolete. This audit uses an analytic front-face roundover with a 14.8 × 14.8 mm footprint. Width comes from the repository’s hardware-facing dimensions; pad length and front-only fillet topology remain assumptions pending CAD confirmation. It is not claimed to be the actual high-fidelity hardware mesh. The back surface is omitted, and the 85° case can be affected by that boundary.

Areas come from the flat face, quarter cylinders and sphere octants. Offsetting sample centers inward changes the local area by 1, (1 − rₛ/r_f), or (1 − rₛ/r_f)² respectively. The foundation modulus stays E/10.55 mm across those regions: a flat face is not assigned stiffness by dividing by local curvature. There is no sharp-edge curvature singularity in this construction.
