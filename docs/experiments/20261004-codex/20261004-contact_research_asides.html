DeliGrasp: physical priors, then analytic translation

Status: speculative backlog note, not an implemented controller or measured result. The proposed connection is to use language/vision for rough physical priors, then use mechanics to obtain simulator parameters. The user's assessment was “seems too far fetched but worth cataloguing.”

DeliGrasp: Inferring Object Properties with LLMs for Adaptive Grasp Policies (Xie, Valentini, Lavering and Correll) estimates mass, friction and a spring constant from semantic object descriptions and uses those estimates in an adaptive grasp policy based on mechanics. Its experiments concern a two-finger gripper and delicate everyday objects. This does not demonstrate fingertip quadrature calibration, inertia identification or MuJoCo parameter prediction.

Proposed extension, inferred from that structure:

image / semantics + geometry
  → priors over mass, density, friction, modulus, hollow/solid construction
  → estimated COM and full inertia tensor
  → current constraint-space inverse inertia from the simulator
  → analytic contact-parameter translation

The language/vision model would predict physical priors with uncertainty, rather than numerical solref/solimp. Object mass and inertia belong to the object model; fingertip modulus belongs to the pad model, and friction belongs to the material pair. A semantic object spring constant is not directly a fingertip Young's modulus. Geometry, material distribution and hollow construction all matter to inertia.

A possible refinement is semantic prior → grasp/lift probe → mass estimate → small rotational excitation → COM/inertia refinement. The suggested 1–2 s interaction budget is a hypothesis, not an established identification result. Mass, COM and all inertia components may not be observable from one grasp and one rotation; appropriate excitation and sensing would have to be demonstrated.

Adaptive Contact-Implicit Model Predictive Control with Online Residual Learning (Huang, Aydinoglu, Jin and Posa) provides a related prior-plus-online-adaptation pattern: it learns a residual of a hybrid contact model while controlling unknown objects. This is supporting context, not evidence that the proposed semantic-to-inertia pipeline will work.

The measured contact audit supplies the qualification: knowing inertia and restoring coefficients at rest does not guarantee independent spring forces during motion. A possible engineering target is an inertia-conditioned MuJoCo surrogate with control equivalence over a declared range of masses, loads, accelerations and slip speeds. That requires held-out control trials and comparison against a physics-only or probe-only baseline; it is not recovered material identification.

Sathya's matrix-free Delassus operator

The user confirmed the intended paper as Matrix-Free Delassus Operations: Scalable and Memory-Efficient Algorithms by Ajay Suresha Sathya, Louis Montaut, Yann de Mont-Marin and Justin Carpentier. It computes products with the Delassus operator and its regularized inverse using articulated dynamics, avoiding explicit matrix construction.

The mathematical connection is direct: the operator is the same J M⁻¹ Jᵀ that the contact audit logs. The proposed benefit is efficient computation and diagnosis of dense-contact coupling, not a different constitutive law. Efficiently applying an operator does not remove physical off-diagonal coupling or make MuJoCo's frictional contact objective equivalent to independent Winkler springs.

Proposed uses: matrix-vector products A x = J[M⁻¹(Jᵀx)] for coupled-contact diagnostics; an efficient linear algebra component if a future solver needs it; and comparisons of full versus reduced patches that preserve resultant wrench but may alter local force modes. Exact diagonal entries can be probed without storing A, but all diagonals generally require multiple queries or a dedicated diagonal algorithm; one operator product is not automatically a complete diagonal.

Our six-DOF audit already exploits a small mass matrix for its reference calculation and saves full normal blocks only at five snapshots. A production articulated-hand implementation would need a scaling benchmark before replacing native MuJoCo routines. The paper's damped-inverse computational advantage does not, by itself, solve unilateral contact, Coulomb friction or quadrature fidelity.

Related earlier work: PV-OSIMr: A Lowest Order Complexity Algorithm for Computing the Delassus Matrix (Sathya, Decré and Swevers), which explicitly constructs the matrix efficiently for kinematic trees. It is a separate result from the matrix-free paper.

Lateral material coupling and tangential friction

Proposal: represent the known fingertip as a deformable surface with local normal compliance, lateral load spreading and elastic shear before slip. Sphere samples, a triangular mesh, a surface grid or tactile locations could discretize the same idea. Material coupling is distinct from rigid-body coupling through the Delassus operator: changing inverse-inertia scaling does not introduce a deforming skin or a tangential displacement history.

Two effects need separate identification: indentation at one location displaces neighboring material; tangential motion stores shear before interfacial sliding. Our rigidly attached native MuJoCo samples have no explicit skin displacement state. MuJoCo's friction constraints do not supply an elastic tangential spring merely by changing solref: the documented friction law is first order, with zero stiffness.

Correction to the commentary's model hierarchy: Compliant Sphere Lattice Contact (CSLC) already solves a quasistatic equilibrium using warm-started damped Jacobi iterations. It has three-dimensional displacement, anchor springs and lateral springs. Its presliding friction saturates smoothly; the authors explicitly describe it as tangentially stateless and exclude kinetic sliding dynamics. Thus CSLC is relevant prior art, but does not establish the proposed rolling shear-history model. An explicitly integrated stiff lattice is a possible implementation, not an inherent CSLC requirement.

Drake's hydroelastic guide explicitly excludes true deformation and tangential compliance. Its pressure patch remains a useful task comparator. Our tested Newton hydroelastic path also needs qualification: contact reduction affected the torsional envelope, and its dynamic readiness gate remains unresolved; see the articulated report and native-state videos. This is not a claim about every Newton solver, nor evidence that any simulator is hardware ground truth.

A reduced surface law and the solve it still needs

Constitutive sketch, not runnable equations: a Winkler/Pasternak-like extension adds a surface coupling operator, while a brush-like shear state stores preslip displacement. In a locally planar basis, one candidate elastic energy and trial resistance are:

Hₙ = Kₙ + αₙ Lₙ                  Hₜ = Kₜ + αₜ Lₜ
E = ½ δₙᵀ Hₙ δₙ + ½ ξᵀ Hₜ ξ
rₙ = Hₙ δₙ + Cₙ δ̇ₙ              fₜ,trial = −Hₜ ξ − Cₜ vₜ
fₙ ≥ 0                            ‖fₜ,ᵢ‖ ≤ μᵢ fₙ,ᵢ

Here δₙ is skin compression, ξ is elastic tangential displacement, and rₙ is the internal normal resistance to balance against contact. It is not automatically the admissible normal contact force. Use positive-semidefinite elastic and damping operators with consistent area and spacing weights; refinement must preserve the physical pad stiffness and coupling length. On a curved fingertip, neighboring tangent vectors need a consistent transport rule before applying Lₜ.

A solver must also close the gap/deformation relation, enforce unilateral separation, evolve ξ through stick and slip, and handle material entering or leaving a migrating patch. Friction opposes sliding; equality at the Coulomb boundary alone does not specify its direction or history update. A coupled return mapping or equivalent constrained solve is needed: independently clipping the trial force at every node does not generally solve the laterally coupled problem. A smooth friction approximation is a separate modeling choice to validate for creep and energy dissipation.

The rolling-contact brush-model literature motivates carrying distributed shear through a moving patch. Applying that idea to a printed fingertip is a research extension, not a demonstrated equivalence to a tire or to calibrated TPU mechanics.

Proposed computational route: eliminate fast elastic inertia by an implicit or quasistatic skin solve, while retaining object dynamics, patch migration and the shear/viscoelastic history that affects control. The supplied 5–50 kHz cell-mode range is an unverified illustration, not a measured property of our print. Fixed topology can permit reuse of the linear elastic operator; changing active contacts and friction still require a state-dependent solve. Sparse GPU iterations and a modal basis δ ≈ Φz with 10–30 modes are candidates, not established speedups.

Implicit stability does not establish accuracy at 10–20 ms. A skin solve followed by a lagged force applied to rigid-body dynamics can itself be unstable; joint solving, coupling iterations or another justified interface treatment must be evaluated. Subcycling only the skin is not sufficient evidence of stable hand–object coupling. Benchmark convergence, energy behavior and wall time together.

Identify the printed TPU, then choose the reduction

eFlesh uses cut-cell microstructures to tune geometry and mechanical response. This supports investigating hardware/model co-design; it does not validate the contact law above. Gyroid infill being approximately isotropic or Winkler-like for this particular print remains unverified. Cell size, walls, print direction, boundaries and loading rate can matter.

Suggested offline programme: measure indentation influence functions at several locations and loads; measure preslip shear and torsional response at fixed normal loads; then measure unloading, rate dependence and hysteresis. Calibrated cell-scale FEM can supplement hardware measurements. Extract normal/tangential stiffness, spatial coupling and damping/friction laws, with uncertainty, and reserve different loads, speeds and contact paths for validation.

The suggested decay u(r) ∼ exp(−r/ℓ) is a fitting hypothesis, not a universal elastic Green's function. Likewise ℓ/rpatch is a useful heuristic: short influence lengths motivate local foundations, patch-sized influence motivates coupled surfaces, and broad influence motivates global modes. Neither proves a particular reduction adequate. Compare measured influence and task errors across mesh resolutions before choosing parameters or mode counts.

The hardware opportunity is to design predictable normal response, lateral coupling, shear and hysteresis that admit a cheap model and useful tactile observations. Keep spherical fingertips as the baseline; any rectangular-tip validation uses the actual 6 mm fillet. The obsolete sharp box is not the hardware geometry. Online MPC latency and batched RL throughput need separate measurements; neither follows from having a small constitutive model.

The supplied hierarchy is preserved in the source archive. Its optimistic reduced-skin timing and pessimistic native-sphere timing are predictions, not measured rankings. The requested sweep stops at 10 ms physics; a direct 20 ms step would be a separately declared extension.

  1. Hold the task fixed. Use the same geometry, mass/inertia, initial state, command trajectory, friction target, grip strategy and actuator limits. Hold control between 20 ms updates; do not retune gains per timestep in the primary comparison. Keep sphere resolution fixed in this sweep; later resolution trials preserve area, pad stiffness and normal load rather than adding unnormalized friction capacity. Retain the full pickup/rolling trial; any common-preload-state diagnostic must be separately labeled.
  2. Separate model choices. Compare legacy spheres, fixed physical mapping and runtime mapping as distinct variants, with diagexact off/on under the same current MuJoCo version where supported. Compare Drake hydroelastic at the same controller period and steps. Record engine version, integrator, solver tolerance/iterations, friction dimensions and actual timestep. Newton joins after its readiness gate passes; an unavailable comparison is not a failed rolling result.
  3. Check what a timestep changes. With positive solref and refsafe enabled, MuJoCo clamps the time constant to at least twice the physics step. A nominal 15 ms time constant becomes 20 ms at a 10 ms step, changing the effective law despite unchanged XML. Log effective coefficients and active clamps; do not silently disable safeguards or retune materials. Report backend stabilization and regularization changes alongside the task metrics.
  4. Judge task behavior. Record roll angle/gain, accumulated interfacial slip distance and slip speed, object position/orientation, contact wrench in a common frame, retention and task success. Aggregate wrench consistently over controller intervals. Compare each backend with its own converged finer-step reference, then compare backends; Drake agreement alone is not physical truth. Declare task-error tolerances and success criteria before running, then perturb preload, mass, command speed and contact phase to test robustness. Local force-law error stays diagnostic.
  5. Measure cost and show motion. Report physics wall time per simulated second, median/p95 controller-interval cost and MPC rollout throughput; separate initialization, compilation and rendering. Report GPU batch size and steady-state throughput separately. Save synchronized native-state videos for all outcomes, including slip, drops and numerical failures, with timestep, controller rate and simulation time visible.

Decision rule: choose the coarsest step meeting the predeclared task and cost tolerances. If a required model only works at 50–100 µs and exceeds the rollout budget, prioritize the reduced skin. Failure of the mapped variant alone is not a reason to abandon the already-working legacy baseline. A 50–100 Hz controller is an operating target; whether each interval can afford the required contact solves remains an empirical question.

Coupled-foundation models for the printed fingertip

Status: not pursued now. Forward planning for the hardware fingertip, which will probably be a TPU print, possibly with gyroid infill. The current work is the 1 mm sphere-packed pads in MuJoCo, scored on task-level friction torque, contact area and tangential friction against Drake and Newton hydroelastic, with simulation speed as the main goal. Per-contact Winkler fidelity is not a target of that work, and nothing in this section changes it.

The sections above carry most of the archived commentary. Four of its items were missing and are recorded here.

  • The named model. The commentary calls the normal law a Pasternak (shear-layer) foundation, fn = Knδn + KℓnLδn + Cnδ̇n with L the surface graph Laplacian, and gives each surface element a tangential state ξi with ft,i = Ktξi + Kℓt(Lξ)i + Ctvt,i until ‖ft,i‖ = μfn,i, where that element slips. This is the Hn, Ht sketch above with α = Kℓ. The surface can be sampled by spheres, a triangle mesh, a UV grid or taxel sites; no tetrahedral mesh of the fingertip is needed.
  • The online parameter set. After an offline identification the reduced model needs kn, kt, ℓn, ℓt, cn, ct and μ, with lookup tables against compression where the print stiffens. The commentary states that homogenized gyroid moduli against infill density are standard in the literature. That claim has not been checked against a source here.
  • The interface to the rigid bodies. (q, q̇) → surface penetration and velocity → fingertip constitutive solve → (F, τ) → rigid-body dynamics. The skin is integrated implicitly or subcycled, so the hand is not stepped at the skin's rate (the commentary's example is 20 kHz). K = KnI + KℓL has fixed topology: it can be factorized once, relaxed with a few Jacobi iterations, run as a GPU stencil, or reduced to 10–30 modes per fingertip (δ ≈ Φz) taken from FEM of the printed tip or from measured influence functions.
  • The intended consumers. A modal skin keeps each MPC contact solve to 10–30 states per fingertip, and the local operator runs as one GPU kernel over surface nodes and worlds for RL. The skin-solve section gives the reasons neither speedup is established.

What it would take on the hardware tip

Our numbers. The hydroelastic law that the 1 mm pads reproduce gives a torsion arm per pad r̄ = 0.996 mm (N / 1 N)1/4 on the real_v1 fingertip (pinch-torque rig, E = 10 MPa, Drake's default rather than a measured TPU modulus). The pressure is paraboloidal, so the contact radius is a = (15/8) r̄. The rig recorded 9–22 spheres in contact per pad.

Where the sphere pads sit. Each pad sphere is a Winkler element. At rest its MuJoCo contact is an independent spring, K = 1 / (tc²(1 − d0) diagApprox), and the per-sphere friction cones give distributed Coulomb slip, so the patch carries the torsion that a point contact lacks (point contact gives zero torque in both simulators on the rig). Indenting one sphere does not load its neighbours, so the pads have no lateral coupling; the coupling the audit measures in motion runs through the Delassus operator, which is rigid-body inertia and not material. The pads also have no elastic tangential pre-slip state. MuJoCo's friction law is first order with zero stiffness, so below the cone the pads creep at a regularized rate instead of storing shear.

Hydroelastic contact and CSLC. Hydroelastic contact is also Winkler-type: the pressure at a point of the contact surface depends only on the local field value, and Drake's guide excludes deformation and tangential compliance. The pads and the hydroelastic patch are therefore one class of model with the same per-area stiffness E/R, which is why the pads reproduce the hydroelastic torque from E alone. This page describes CSLC as a three-dimensional displacement per sphere with anchor and lateral springs, solved quasistatically by damped Jacobi iterations, with presliding friction that saturates smoothly and no tangential state by its authors' account. On that description the pads and the hydroelastic patch correspond to CSLC's anchor springs alone, and CSLC's lateral springs are the KℓL term.

All four keywords exist in 3.14.0: full, radial, trilinear and quadratic; an unknown keyword fails at parse. Under integrator="discrete" every compiled skin settled on the box with the pressing force carried and zero tip speed. The 193-vertex radial skin (192 sliders; one vertex has none) costs 36 µs per step, the 521-vertex one 102 µs, and the trilinear skin 16 µs with 24 DOFs. Under implicitfast the explicit slider springs chattered: after 3 s the radial skin still moved at 115 mm/s and held 50 contacts, the mjMAXCONPAIR cap per geom pair, at 97 µs per step.

Contact stiffness decides whether the skin holds at all. MuJoCo's contact stiffness scales with the effective mass, and with the box's default solref and solimp mixed in, a 5 mg vertex contact is near 1 N/m; the first probe fell through the box. The probe puts the normal compliance in the slider springs and stiffens the contact with solimp 0.9999 on both the skin and the box, because solmix averages the two geoms' parameters. A calibrated skin would need the audit's per-contact compensation instead.

None of the native coupling options tested gives Pasternak-type load spreading on a moving finger. Edge stiffness exists only for dim=1 flexes. Shell stretch is rejected under implicit and implicitfast and runs under discrete (49 µs per step), and with radial DOFs alone its first-order strain along an edge on a sphere is (wi + wj)/2R, which adds local hoop stiffness and does not spread an indentation to the neighbours; this follows from the kinematics and was not measured. Shell bending, which would give a plate-on-springs coupling length (D/k)1/4, did not compile with radial DOFs on the jointed tip body or on a jointless mount body. The remaining native option is a passive-force callback (mjcb_passive) that applies KℓL to the slider coordinates; its stability at 1 ms would need its own test. A skin at the pads' 1 mm spacing also needs a cap mesh over the 35° pad, about 126 vertices, rather than the whole sphere, which the two ellipsoids above sample at about 2.7 and 1.6 mm.

Measurements that decide it on the printed tip. Indent one site with a probe much smaller than a (0.5 mm tip) at 0.5, 1 and 3 N and record the displacement u(r) of the surrounding surface, by DIC on a speckled tip or by a second probe stepped outward. Fit u(r) ∼ exp(−r/ℓ) and compare ℓ with a = 1.6–2.5 mm. Repeat at two indentation rates for hysteresis, and once over a lattice wall and once over a void: a gyroid period comparable to the 3.1–4.9 mm patch diameter would make the law depend on where the patch lands, and no homogenized surface model would hold. A torsion sweep on the pinch rig over 0.5–3 N measures the task quantity directly. The arm r̄ grows by 61/4 = 1.57 over that range under a Winkler-type law and by 61/3 = 1.82 under a Hertzian half-space, where the whole surface is coupled. A shear test at fixed normal load gives kt and the pre-slip displacement before gross slip.

What would trigger the implementation. Either ℓ above about 0.5 a on the chosen TPU or gyroid, which is 0.8 mm at 0.5 N and 1.2 mm at 3 N, or a task metric that the pad model misses against hardware, such as the rolling ratio or pre-slip creep under a sustained tangential load. A torsion-arm exponent clearly above 1/4 in the sweep counts as such a miss.

When to revisit

The next proposed discriminator is task accuracy versus computational cost at a fixed controller period, before committing to a new contact model. A reduced skin becomes attractive if measured shear memory or lateral deformation matters to the task and the existing backends cannot reproduce it within the rollout budget. Revisit semantic priors if physical probing is limited or object initialization becomes a demonstrated bottleneck. Revisit the matrix-free implementation if articulated dense-contact operator construction dominates runtime or memory. Neither aside changes the current fingertip material parameters or controller.

Measured runtime-inertia and controlled-slip results are the prerequisite evidence. The articulated torsion, holding and CPU/GPU follow-up now separates physical stiffness mapping, exact diagonals, finite tangential creep and backend cost. Its CPU profile does not construct a dense Delassus matrix in the timed loop; matrix-free work is not yet demonstrated as the dominant bottleneck. The archived inertia mapping discussion and speed/fidelity discussion are interpreted in that report.
