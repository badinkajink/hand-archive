Servo plant refit, hand-object control of the three-finger turn, and the cost of sphere-pad fingertips in RL training

The deployed open-loop three-finger turn holds the tool on 40 of 40 placements and turns it 27–57° in MuJoCo once the scene carries the servo response the bench readbacks show (0.02 s instead of 1 s), friction near 1 and the tool resting on its post. The relative contact-velocity controller of Wang, Oh and Pollard, keeping all three contacts and regulating pad force, holds 40 of 40 placements but stops at 6–42°, because the deployed grips leave the pips no extension; without its stop rule it drops the tool on 6 of 8 hands. Sphere pads at 1 mm cost 12 % of RL env throughput at 2,048 envs, because physics is a small part of an env step; from one held RL state the pads cost 2.2–2.4 µs of physics per world-step in MuJoCo-Warp, Newton’s hydroelastic contact on the plain fingertip shape 4.3 µs, point contact 0.6–1.4 µs. SolverMuJoCo realises Newton’s hydroelastic stiffness times the tip–tool effective mass; with kh divided by that mass the deployed plans hold the tool on 35 of 40 placements in Newton (10 uncorrected) and turn it within 5° of MuJoCo on four of eight hands. The 2026-09-02 servo-gain fit closed the fingers on air; the bench readbacks fix the finger time constant and favour μ 1 but do not identify the gain.

Terms and metrics

tool turn
rotation of the tool’s axis from its pose at the end of the grip, in the vertical plane through the axis, in degrees; positive toward vertical, 90° = standing on end. The open-loop replays measure it from the cosine of the axis with vertical, which equals the same angle for a tool starting flat.
held
at the end of the rollout the tool is within 20 mm of its height at the end of the grip and at least two fingers touch it.
held peak
the largest tool turn reached while the tool was held in that sense (any time in the rollout).
joint readback error
mean over the nine finger joints and over the bench’s three servo readbacks of one run (before the turn, during it, at the settled hold) of |simulated − bench achieved angle|, in degrees; the replay applies the run’s own logged command frames at their logged times.
kp
servo position gain of the simulated finger actuator, N m/rad: torque = kp (command − angle), clipped at the torque limit.
time constant τ
finger joint damping divided by kp, in seconds: the time a free finger joint takes to cover 63 % of a step command (actuator velocity gain 0).
μ
Coulomb friction coefficient of the fingertip pads on the tool.
pads
the printed TPU block (17 × 14.8 × 22 mm, 2.7 mm fillets on the front and end edges) covered with 0.75 mm spheres at 1 mm spacing (1,059 per tip; 290 at 2 mm); stiffness E/h per sphere area with E 10 MPa, h 8.5 mm, reached at load through solimp.
HOM controller
the relative contact-velocity controller of Wang, Oh and Pollard (arXiv 2609.25619): per fingertip, a contact frame from the closest points of tip and tool, joint rates from bounded least squares on reference relative velocities (their Eq. 2). Defined in Section 3.
governor
the HOM controller’s stop rule: when a pad’s normal force falls below 30 % of its target or the tool lags the reference by more than 6°, the reference stops and the angle reached is held.
inverse weight
MuJoCo’s body_invweight0[b, 0]: the translational inverse inertia of body b at the model’s reference pose, in 1/kg, computed at compile time from the joint-space mass matrix; 1/mass for a free body, and for a fingertip the inverse of the inertia the whole finger chain and its servo armature present at the tip.
effective mass meff
of a contact between bodies 1 and 2: 1/(inverse weight of 1 + inverse weight of 2), in kg. MuJoCo’s solref is a spring on the constraint acceleration, so a contact realises its solref stiffness times meff as a force stiffness.
sink
steady penetration of each pad into the tool in the contact bed’s static pinch, in mm, after 1 s at the set normal force with gravity off; Drake hydroelastic gives 0.153, 0.213 and 0.365 mm at 0.5, 1 and 3 N.
onset torque
contact bed, twist task: a torque about the pinch axis ramps up on the pinched tool; the onset torque is the last value at which the spin speed still follows the creep law w = k τ, in mN m. Drake hydroelastic gives 0.84, 1.97 and 7.65 mN m at 0.5, 1 and 3 N.
env steps/s
policy steps per second summed over all parallel environments of the RL training env; one env step is 10 physics steps of 2 ms plus observations, rewards and terminations.
physics µs per world-step
wall time of one batched physics step divided by the number of worlds.

Bench post and servo plant

The post under the tool

Scene.set_object_platform(height) now puts the post’s top at the tool’s lowest point, computed from the compiled geometry (height − 12.5 mm for the flat 25 mm tool), and keeps height as the tool’s centre, which the exported plans’ grip poses refer to. Stored scenes are repaired on load by seated_scene(path), which writes <stem>__seated.xml beside the original. plant_drop_gate.py, real_v1_bench_plants.py, real_v1_turn_mechanism.py, plant_ranking_test.py, real_v1_plan_angle_sweep.py, real_v1_tip_gait.py and calibrate_plant_kp.py load scenes through it. In the D7 scene the post top moved from 100.0 to 87.5 mm and the tool’s initial penetration of the post from 12.5 mm to 0.

The 2026-09-02 gain fit never loaded a finger

calibrate_plant_kp.py reset each candidate scene from its keyframe 0, which puts the palm at 10 mm; the plan’s replay state puts it at 103.5 mm, above the tool on its 100 mm post. The fingers closed on air while the palm rested against the tool and the post, so the +11.09° middle-yaw deficit that matched the bench’s +11.68° at kp 0.5 came from the fingers’ own response, with no load from the tool (time constant 1.04 s at kp 0.5 with the template’s joint damping 0.5, read 2 s after the last command). The script now starts from the plan’s replay state; its fit is superseded by the replays below.

The readbacks fix two things. The finger time constant is short: τ at or below 0.2 s gives 2.53–2.63° and 0.6 s gives 3.60°, which rules out the 1 s fingers every calibrated-plant result since 2026-09-16 ran on. Friction near 1 fits better than the scenes’ 2.4 by about 1°. The servo gain is not identified: across kp 0.25–30 the best error stays within 2.53–2.75°, because a finger pressing on the tool stops at the tool, and its deficit is set by the grip geometry, whatever the stiffness behind it. The torque limit is not identified either. The fit’s cost also included the tool’s tracked tilt, and with it the best plants had kp 2–10 and μ 0.8–1.3; bench task outcomes depend on how the tool was placed and on contacts the scene does not model, so that part of the fit is not used here.

The rest of this page runs the working plant: kp 4 N m/rad, τ 0.02 s (joint damping 0.08), torque limit 1 N m, no joint friction, μ 1.0 (reorient_backends plant spec kp4_kv0_fr1_fl0_dp0.08). A gain measurement needs a known torque on a joint (Section 6).

Relative contact-velocity control of the three-finger turn

The question was whether the hand-object controls of Wang, Oh and Pollard can carry out our reorientation, in which all three fingers move the tool together, in place of the deployed open-loop joint trajectories. The scene is the bench maneuver of each deployed hand D1–D8: palm fixed, the 25 mm, 24.5 g tool lying on its post, the plan’s grip held 0.8 s, the working plant, the TPU tip as 1 mm pads in MuJoCo and as a compliant convex (E 10 MPa, relaxation 0.01 s) in Drake. Placements: seed 0 at the plan’s pose, seeds 1–4 jittered by 2 mm and 2° (MuJoCo 5 placements per hand, Drake 3).

Controller

At 100 Hz the controller reads the nine joint angles and the tool pose, poses a kinematic MuJoCo copy of the scene with the TPU block as one convex mesh per tip, and builds the paper’s contact frame per finger: origin at the closest points of block and tool (mj_geomDistance), x the tool’s surface normal toward the finger, y the finger’s flexion axis projected on the tangent plane. The paper commands relative contact velocities (finger minus tool at the contact). For a turn that keeps all three contacts, each contact’s reference is the velocity of the tool’s material point under the desired tool motion, so the relative velocity is zero while the tool follows, plus a normal term that regulates the pad force:

ωd = a0 (dθref/dt + KR(θref − θ)) + KR (u × uref),  vd = KP (cref − c)
vi = vd + ωd × (pi − c) + ni KF (Fi − Fd)

Here θ is the tool turn, a0 the horizontal axis normal to the starting tool axis, u the tool axis and uref the starting axis turned by θref about a0, c the tool’s material point at the starting centroid of the three contacts (held 5 mm above its start), pi and ni the contact point and normal of finger i, Fi its pad force from the simulator’s contact solution and Fd = 2 N. KR = KP = 3 s−1, KF = 0.03 m/(s N). The paper’s Eq. 2 then gives each finger’s joint rates by bounded least squares on the three translational rows (weight 1, scaled by 1/20 mm) and the three rotational rows (weight 0.01), with joint-rate damping 10−3, joint limits and |rate| ≤ 2 rad/s. The servo targets integrate the rates and stay within 0.3 rad of the measured angles. The reference turns at 30°/s toward vertical after a 0.5 s squeeze in which the plan’s 5–13 N grip relaxes to 2 N, and the rollout ends 2 s after the reference stops. Code: scripts/hom_turn3.py; the same controller drives MuJoCo and Drake.

Grasp search by turn range

The turn range of a grasp is the largest rotation of the tool toward vertical, about the horizontal axis normal to its starting axis through the contacts’ centroid (the rotation the controller commands), at which, for every smaller angle in 3° steps, each fingertip still reaches its tool-fixed contact point (inverse kinematics of the finger’s three joints puts its pad point within 1 mm of the contact, inside the joint limits less a margin, with the pad normal within 45° of the tool’s surface normal) and pad forces exist that hold the tool (a linear program over the three contact forces: they balance the tool’s weight and moments, lie in the μ 1 friction pyramid with at least 0.5 N normal, and load no finger joint beyond the 1 N m servo limit). The grasp is the tool’s offset on its post (±12 mm along its axis, ±5 mm across, ±15° yaw) and each finger’s contact on the cylinder (position along the axis and angle around it); CEM with 96 samples, 8 elites and 15 iterations maximises the range from the deployed grip’s contacts (scripts/hom_grasp_search.py, rows grasp_search.jsonl, turn3_best_grasp.jsonl). The controller then starts from the searched grasp: grip targets from the inverse kinematics at 0° with each pad commanded 2 mm into the tool, fingers starting with the pads 8 mm outside their contacts (from the plan’s open pose, the 3°-margin grasps knocked the tool off its post).

Effective-mass scaling of Newton’s hydroelastic stiffness

Newton passes each hydroelastic contact point to MuJoCo-Warp with a force stiffness c in N/m (the point’s share of the pressure field: patch area times the two shapes’ kh in series). SolverMuJoCo writes it as solref time constant √(1/(c(1−d))), damping ratio 1 and solimp (d, d, 0.001, 1, 0.5) (newton/_src/solvers/mujoco/kernels.py, lines 599–618 at Newton commit 009158e). MuJoCo treats solref as a spring on the constraint acceleration, so the contact holds c × meff per metre of penetration, not c. Newton’s shape-material path multiplies its stiffness by the inverse-weight sum before the same conversion (docs/solvers/mujoco.rst, “Shape-material contact stiffness and damping”); the hydroelastic path does not, and the ShapeConfig.kh docstring (newton/_src/sim/builder.py) says that SolverMuJoCo scales the stiffness by masses and that kh should be tuned with that in mind. The MuJoCo sphere pads already divide meff out: their solimp d0 = 1 − 1/(tc2 K (wtip + wtool)) uses the same inverse weights (reorient_backends.replace_tips), which is why they matched Drake without fitting. The correction is therefore kh × (w1 + w2), with the inverse weights read from the solver’s own MuJoCo model (solver.mj_model.body_invweight0 through solver.mjc_body_to_newton); kh = E/h stays a material constant.

On the 2026-10-05 contact bed (two 20 g pads on rails pinching the 24.5 g tool, rows docs/experiments/20261006-newton_mass_scaling/, contact_bed_newton.py models newton_hydro_mc*) the solver’s model gives each pad an inverse weight of 16.7 1/kg and the tool 40.7, so meff = 17.4 g and uncorrected Newton sank 7.1–7.4 times as deep as Drake with 2.4–2.6 times Drake’s onset torque. Multiplied by 57.4 1/kg, Newton sinks 1.02–1.04 times Drake’s depth and reaches 0.76–0.88 of its onset torque, with no parameter fitted. Inverse weights from a separate MuJoCo compile of the bed’s MJCF give a factor of 90.7 and over-stiffen the contact (sink 0.82–0.83 times Drake), so the factor has to come from the solver’s model.

Cost of sphere-pad fingertips in RL training

The RL comparison asked for here trains the reorientation with the pad fingertip and with the point contact every run so far has used, then replays the policies in Drake and Newton. Before training, the cost. The environment is the D6 reorientation env of the RL pipeline (mjlab on MuJoCo-Warp 3.6: scripted grasp and lift, 2 ms step, 10 physics steps per policy step, elliptic cone, impratio 10, 10 solver and 20 line-search iterations) on morphology runs that differ only in the fingertip (make_pad_morphology_run.py). The pads touch only the tool; the block’s convex mesh takes the floor and finger-to-finger contacts (with pads colliding with everything, a 2 mm pad tip resting on the floor made up to 738 contacts per world). Contact sensor for sphere pads

The fingertip and palm contact sensors now sum every matched contact (mjlab reduce="netforce", a world-frame vector) and the simulation allocates 256 matches per sensor (contact_sensor_maxmatch; mjlab’s default of 64 overflowed with the pads), set in src/morphohand/rl/env_build.py. Before, each fingertip reported one contact chosen by match order (reduce="none", one slot): the whole force of the box tip, one pad’s share on the pad tip. Every consumer (grip-force, force-excess, force-spread and brace rewards, contact gates, tip-lost terminations, the deploy read-outs) takes the vector norm and found > 0, so the change of frame does not reach them. Regression (scripts/rl_contact_sensor_check.py, rows docs/experiments/20261006-rl_contact/sensor_check.jsonl): on the box tip the old and new sensors, read side by side in one env for 200 steps at 64 envs, give every sensor-reading reward term the same value (largest difference 0) and force norms within 1.9e-06 N; no fingertip had more than one matched contact. On the 1 mm pads (8 envs, 4 instants after the grasp, 96 fingertip samples, up to 31 pad contacts per tip) the sensor equals the sum of the pad contact forces of the same GPU solve to 2e-07 and a CPU MuJoCo re-solve of the same state to 0.2 %. At 2,048 envs the env runs 9,295 (box tip) and 7,912 (pads) env steps/s with the summed sensor, against 9,439 and 8,329 in Table 6 with the one-contact sensor.

The env has no mass randomisation. Adding one requires recomputing each pad’s solimp d0 per world, since d0 holds the inverse weights of the nominal tip and tool. The compliance randomisation (randomize_geom_solimp) overwrites d0 and is off in the configuration the training below copies.

Physics cost on one held RL state

Each engine starts from the same state of the RL env: D6 after its scripted grasp and lift (60 zero-action policy steps, 64 worlds, every tool held; with 1 mm pads 41 contacts and 132 constraint rows per world), exported with mjlab’s scene writer (scripts/rl_state_export.py; free-body positions relative to each world’s grid origin, which mjlab adds per world). Every engine loads that MJCF with each body’s compiled inertia pinned, the trainer’s options (2 ms, implicitfast, elliptic cone, impratio 10, 10 solver and 20 line-search iterations), the exported joint angles tiled over the batch, the exported servo targets and zero velocity, and steps 22 graph-captured blocks of 50 steps (scripts/same_state_timing.py, rows docs/experiments/20261006-rl_contact/same_state_timing.jsonl). Newton 1.7.0.dev0 uses its own collision pipeline (contact gap 0.5 mm, margin 0), the TPU block re-added as a mesh from its OBJ, and for hydroelastic contact kh = E/h on the tips multiplied by the tip–tool 1/meff of its solver model (Section 4; tip inverse weights 4.95–5.14, tool 39.1 1/kg), 100 times that on the tool, 0.5 mm grid, ±6 mm band, contact reduction, buffers at 1.3 % of Newton’s defaults. The pads’ solimp d0 in Newton is recomputed from its solver model’s inverse weights, which equal the MJCF compile’s to 10−8, so d0 is unchanged. The 1 mm pads cost 2.41 µs per world-step in MuJoCo-Warp 3.6 from this state (mjlab’s env 2.98) and hold every tool. The 17–26 µs and 13 % drops measured earlier on the bench grip came from two settings of the raw MuJoCo-Warp script: rerun with both corrected, the bench grip costs 3.19 µs with 122 contacts per world and holds 100 % (rows newton_throughput.jsonl). First, it forced the SAP_SEGMENTED broadphase; MuJoCo-Warp’s put_model, and therefore mjlab, chooses NXN over the contype-filtered geom pairs when they number under 250,000, and the pads touch only the tool, so the pair list is short. On this state SAP costs 16.7 µs. Second, the broadphase writes its candidate pairs into the per-world contact buffer, and on the pads they exceed 250 per world for about 43 contacts; a buffer of 123 contacts per world overflowed and dropped pad contacts, and 52 % of worlds lost the tool. With 512 contacts per world both versions hold every tool; MuJoCo-Warp 3.14 takes 1.75 µs.

Newton’s mass-corrected hydroelastic tip holds every tool at 4.25 µs per world-step (1,024 worlds; 4.34 at 2,048, 8,351 MB of GPU) with 41 reduced contact points and 131 constraint rows per world: 1.8 times the MuJoCo-Warp pads at 1,024 worlds and 4.5–6.8 times point contact on the box tip at 2,048. Newton with sphere pads takes 4.00 µs; its 0.5 mm contact gap reports 259 pad contacts per world, of which about as many become constraint rows as in MuJoCo-Warp. Newton’s point contact on the block mesh loses the tool in 51 % of worlds at 2,048, as it did at 4,096 on the bench grip.
